Halfway Up the Mountain

by Kiran Khalap

✍ Shweta Suryavanshi 📅 November 17, 2026 ⏱ 3 min read
Author Kiran Khalap
Language English
Published 2003 (India), 2005 (UK & US)
Genre Literary Fiction, Spiritual Fiction
Setting Rural India — A Mountain Village & a Traditional Family
Themes Mediocrity, Fate, Womanhood, Spirituality, Loss, Longing

Summary

Halfway Up the Mountain is Kiran Khalap's first novel, and it follows Maya, a girl from a mountain village who is determined not to let tradition write her whole story. Her life becomes intertwined with men of exceptional talent — the kind of people others look up to — and the book itself is often described as a tale of snakebites, separation, and sadness. It is a compact novel, barely over two hundred pages, but it carries a great deal inside that smallness.

What makes the story unusual is how it blends the everyday with the spiritual. Prayer, belief, and hard reality sit side by side on the page, so Maya's struggles with fate and family never feel like a case study; they feel lived. As she comes closer to the gifted men around her, she begins to notice something quietly unsettling: talent and greatness are not the same thing as completeness. Up close, even the extraordinary turn out to be thoroughly human.

The Wisdom of the Middle

The title carries the heart of the book. The word "mediocre" traces back to Latin roots meaning the middle of a rugged mountain — and Khalap has spoken about using exactly this idea. To be mediocre, then, is not to be worthless; it is to be somewhere on the climb, neither at the base nor at the summit. Read that way, the novel becomes a gentle argument against our habit of dividing people into the brilliant and the ordinary.

Maya looks up to the talented men in her life, and the book lets her discover that they are stuck on the mountain too, each at their own ledge, each still reaching. There is relief in that realisation, and a little grief. If even the admired are unfinished, then perhaps being unfinished is simply what it means to be alive, and the climb matters more than the view from any one spot.

"The people we look up to are not standing at the top. They are only standing a little higher on the same slope, still short of breath." — A line from my reading notes, on Halfway Up the Mountain

What Lingers

I finished this book feeling oddly comforted. So much of what we read and watch tells us to chase the summit, and to measure ourselves against people who seem to have already arrived. Khalap's novel quietly refuses that race. It suggests that halfway is not a failure of nerve but an honest place to stand, and that most of life happens there.

It also stayed with me as a portrait of a woman holding on to her own sense of self within a world that had other plans for her. The sadness in the book is real and it is not rushed past, yet it never turns bleak. If you enjoy short, reflective novels that ask big questions softly, this one is worth an afternoon — and likely a few evenings of thinking afterwards.
