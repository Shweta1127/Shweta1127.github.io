Halfway Up the Mountain

by Kiran Khalap

✍ Shweta Suryavanshi 📅 November 17, 2026 ⏱ 3 min read
Author Kiran Khalap
Language English
Published 2003 (India), 2005 (UK & US)
Genre Literary Fiction, Spiritual Fiction
Setting The Konkan, Mumbai & Mahabaleshwar, India
Themes Attachment, Letting Go, Resilience, Grief, Fate, Spirituality

Summary

Halfway Up the Mountain is Kiran Khalap's first novel. It follows Maya, a girl from a Konkan village who is determined not to let tradition write her whole story. Her life becomes intertwined with people of exceptional talent: a poet she has admired since childhood, her world famous painter husband, and a gifted musician who is her son. The book is often described as a tale of snakebites, separation, and sadness. It is barely over two hundred pages, yet it carries a great deal inside that smallness.

What makes the story unusual is how it blends the everyday with the spiritual. Prayer, belief, and hard reality sit side by side on the page, so Maya's struggles with fate and family feel lived rather than studied. She helps the people closest to her find their muse, and in return she is left with the sadness of separation and the gossip of a society that judges her place in their lives. Through all of it she stands firm, and kindness guides every connection she makes.

The Wisdom of the Middle

The title carries the heart of the book. "Mediocre" traces back to Latin roots meaning the middle of a rugged mountain, and Khalap has spoken about using exactly this idea. To be mediocre, then, is not to be worthless; it is to be somewhere on the climb, neither at the base nor at the summit. The novel applies that idea to Maya herself. Next to a celebrated painter husband and a gifted musician son, she is the ordinary one, and the book quietly suggests that this ordinariness is where the real work of living happens.

"She did not walk away from the world to let go of it. She let go from inside it." — A line from my reading notes, on Halfway Up the Mountain

What Lingers

I finished the book with a sense of life's sourness and a deep sadness about separation. The novel suggests that our attachments are among the greatest causes of grief, and that letting them go can set a person free. But Maya is no monk. She never leaves society behind; she stays within it and frees herself by loosening her hold on the people around her. That is why the book feels so honest to me: she seems to be only halfway to liberation, still climbing the mountain of knowledge.

The sadness is real and never rushed past, yet it does not turn bleak. If you enjoy short, reflective novels that ask big questions softly, this one is worth an afternoon and likely a few evenings of thinking afterwards.
